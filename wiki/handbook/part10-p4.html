Chapter 18PMK-ch18-

One thread, two queues

Scheduling around a library that cannot be parallelised or interrupted

pdfium-render stores its bindings in a process-global cell. The application must never create a second Pdfium binding, and PDFium handles must not be shared across threads. Everything in this chapter follows from those two sentences.

core/src/pdf.rs
pub struct PdfRenderer {
    sender: Sender<PdfCommand>,               // standard work
    priority_sender: Sender<PriorityRender>,  // the page the reader is looking at
    visible_range: Arc<Mutex<Option<(u16, u16, String)>>>,
}

PdfRenderer::new() spawns one worker thread that owns the binding and one cached open document. Every PDF operation in the process is serialized onto it. Since parallelism is off the table, the only lever left is ordering — and the design spends it entirely on one thing: the page the reader is looking at right now.

18.1 The scheduling rules

  1. Receive a standard command; drain any others already available into pending_commands.
  2. Before each standard command, drain the priority channel. If several priority requests are queued, only the newest is rendered — the reader has already scrolled past the others.
  3. Process exactly one standard command, then repeat.

PdfCommand::Wake exists solely to unblock receiver.recv() after a priority request is sent; it performs no work of its own.

18.2 The command surface

Standard channel: PageCount, ExtractText, PageSizes, RenderPage, GetToc, GetReferences, GetEmbeddedToc, GetLinks, SearchText, RenderLinkPreview, GetPageText, Wake.
Priority channel: PriorityRender { path, page_index, scale, resp }.

Every command carries a SyncSender for its reply, so the async caller on the Tokio side blocks on a channel rather than on PDFium. That is the seam that keeps the FFI on one thread while still letting the rest of the app await results normally.

Trap Because a command cannot be interrupted, the duration of the slowest command is the worst-case latency of every page render. This is not theoretical: extracting the text of a whole textbook in one ExtractText takes hundreds of milliseconds, and while it runs the reader is looking at a blank page. Chapter 21 shows the fix — chunk the work into per-page commands so priority renders interleave.
Chapter 19PMK-ch19-

Two coordinate systems

PDF measures from the bottom; screens measure from the top

Half the confusing bugs in a PDF viewer are one of these two conventions applied in the other one's space. The codebase's answer is not “convert everything” but “know which space each thing lives in, and convert at exactly one place”.

19.1 Which space, and why it matters

  • Annotation and search rectangles stay in bottom-left PDF points, and are converted at draw time by search_rect_to_view_rect.
  • Reference underlines are already top-left and convert with a plain * zoom.
  • Page text geometry is stored in PDF space, so it survives zoom changes.
Why it is like this Rectangles are stored in PDF points rather than pixels, and that one decision is why a highlight you made last month at 100% zoom on a 1× display still lands on the right words today at 175% zoom on a HiDPI screen in a narrow split pane. Store a pixel rectangle and you have stored an accident of the window you happened to have open.
Chapter 20PMK-ch20-

Finding a table of contents that isn't there

Four sources, in order of trustworthiness, and two guards against inventing one

Many PDFs — especially preprints and scanned books — carry no embedded bookmarks at all, and a reader without an outline is a reader who scrolls. get_toc(path) returns (Vec<TocEntry>, synthetic) and works down a cascade of increasingly speculative sources.

Two entry points exist: recover_toc(doc, &pages) runs the whole chain, and recover_toc_from_texts(&pages) runs the text-only subset with no document handle — which is what the reference resolver uses (Chapter 21), so it does not have to scan the document twice.
